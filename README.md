# Bayesically-Unstoppable---Datafest-2025
As our group began to explore possible directions for this project, we knew that we wanted to work with data that was both substantial in size and rich in variables. Our goal was to find a topic where economic indicators and market-level dynamics could be combined to produce meaningful forecasts. Ultimately, we chose to investigate the relationship between macroeconomic factors and U.S. office rents, both nationally and across major cities, using lease data from 2018 through 2024. We believed this would allow us to test different modeling approaches and compare their strengths in explaining and forecasting rent movements.

To start, we focused on national rent trends. Our initial ridge regression model explained about 61% of the variation in-sample, but it did not generalize well, as it performed poorly out-of-sample. This suggested that rents have strong inertia and that short-term changes in inflation, GDP growth, or stock returns are not sufficient to predict national rent movements on their own. We then turned to a SARIMAX time-series model that combined autoregressive components with macroeconomic indicators. This approach better captured the rent plateau after 2022 and correctly anticipated the cooling in 2024. From this, we concluded that rents adjust gradually, and time-series structure is critical to capturing their dynamics.

Next, we shifted our attention to the city level. We estimated a panel regression with fixed effects, which allowed us to control for differences in average rent levels across markets while estimating common sensitivities to macroeconomic factors. This model performed extremely well, with an adjusted R² of 0.97 across 270 observations. The results highlighted significant variation across cities: Manhattan had intercepts above $75 per square foot, while cities such as Salt Lake City and Phoenix were closer to $25 per square foot. We also found that stock performance and inflation generally pushed rents higher, although the impacts of interest rates and volatility were less consistent across markets.

Finally, we implemented a Bayesian hierarchical model. This approach allowed each city to have its own sensitivity to GDP and stock returns while borrowing strength from the overall distribution through partial pooling. The results reinforced many of our earlier findings. San Francisco and Manhattan had GDP sensitivities that were credibly below average, while Phoenix and Atlanta were more responsive. Stock returns had broadly positive effects across all cities, with New York showing the strongest link to financial markets.

Across all of our modeling approaches, one consistent theme emerged: financial sentiment, captured through stock returns and volatility, tends to influence leasing activity more quickly than GDP or inflation. GDP growth was generally associated with higher rents, but the effect often lagged. In Sunbelt cities, rents rose quickly during market rallies and GDP rebounds, while in San Francisco and Manhattan, rents were decoupled from national GDP trends, likely reflecting local challenges in tech and shifting demand patterns.

In summary, our findings suggest that while macroeconomic indicators are useful in explaining broad rent trends, their predictive power improves significantly when combined with city-specific models and time-series dynamics. We recommend a two-tiered strategy: using national models for broad outlooks while relying on city-level models for more precise forecasting, capital deployment, and risk assessment.
